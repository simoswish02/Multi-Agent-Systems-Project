\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\subsection{Conclusions}
\label{subsec:conc-conclusions}

This project set out to build, analyse, and evaluate a cooperative
multi-agent system for a deceptively simple task --- find a hidden target on a
grid --- made genuinely hard by three coupled sources of uncertainty: partial
observability, limited communication, and, as its defining extension, the
possibility that any drone permanently breaks at any moment with no central
authority to announce the loss. We summarize what was accomplished along the
three axes the report developed.

\paragraph{What was built.}
We implemented, from scratch, a configurable cooperative grid environment
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) with sequential turn-based execution,
accumulated per-drone knowledge maps, range-limited map fusion, and a
decentralized fault model in which crash knowledge propagates only through a
passive distress beacon and ordinary communication --- never a global oracle
(Section~\ref{subsec:prob-fault}). A single Dueling Double DQN with parameter
sharing (Section~\ref{subsec:arch-network}) drives every drone through one
shared forward pass; per-episode domain randomization together with a compact
context vector (Section~\ref{subsec:arch-context}) makes that one policy a
generalist across team size, sensing, communication, clutter, and fault rate.
The training methodology (Section~\ref{sec:training}) treated the fault model
with the care value-based learning demands --- most consequentially, that a
fault is a \emph{truncation}, not a terminal, so bad luck is never charged to
the value function.

\paragraph{What the multi-agent reading revealed.}
Section~\ref{sec:mas} read the system through the vocabulary of the field and
found that its most interesting properties are precisely the ones no line of
code states directly. Coordination is achieved \emph{without} protocols,
performatives, or a coordinator: dispersion from a shared spawn corner is
driven by environment-mediated, stigmergic repulsion from swept regions; role
differentiation among identical drones is broken open by a single identity
scalar; and the ``knowledge'' each drone acts on is a first-order epistemic
belief --- sound but possibly stale --- updated by observation and by
testimony from teammates. The fault handling, in particular, is an epistemic
story: graceful degradation is a system-level property that no individual
drone implements, resting on redundancy and decentralization rather than on
any drone knowing the true state of the team.

\paragraph{What the experiments showed.}
The evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) substantiated the central
claims and bounded them honestly. A single policy generalized across every
randomization axis, including \emph{beyond} its trained ranges on team size,
vision, and communication --- driving teams of six drones it never trained on
at over $90\%$ success. Most importantly, degradation under faults is
\emph{graceful}: across the entire trained fault range success fell by under
six points while the team shed, on average, more than half a drone, and even
at a fault rate more than triple the trained maximum --- losing roughly a
third of the team per episode --- the system still completed two missions in
three, with no cliff. The evaluation was equally clear about limits: obstacle
density is the one axis whose extrapolation collapses; communication, on this
task, refines coordination but does not create it (severing it costs almost
nothing in find rate); and the emergent-coordination and belief-lag claims are
only partially instrumented so far.

\paragraph{What the ethical analysis concluded.}
Section~\ref{sec:ethics} argued that the very capability studied here is
irreducibly dual-use --- ``find a hidden target in minimum time'' is
deployment-agnostic --- and that the fault tolerance which makes a
search-and-rescue swarm robust is the same property that would make an attack
swarm resilient. The analysis mapped the civilian system onto GDPR and the EU
AI Act, and confronted the military carve-out (Art.~2(3)) that lets the war
scenario escape the Act entirely, before arguing that \emph{this specific
system} --- opaque, statistically rather than formally validated, acting on
stale beliefs under partial observability, with no single decision locus ---
would be unacceptable as an autonomous weapon on exactly the grounds its own
evaluation exposes. The takeaway is not that the technology should not be
built, but that its governance cannot be an afterthought to its engineering.

Taken together, the contribution is a small but complete study: a working
fault-tolerant cooperative system, a systematic multi-agent reading that
grounds each abstract concept in a concrete mechanism, an experimental
characterization of what the learned policy does and does not generalize, and
an ethical analysis that takes the dual-use nature of the result seriously.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{subsec:conc-future}

The limitations of Section~\ref{subsec:exp-discussion} map onto a concrete
agenda, which we group by how far it reaches from the present system.

\paragraph{Completing the present evaluation.}
Three measurements were defined in this report but not carried out, each for a
mundane reason. The belief-lag question --- how communication range governs
the interval between a fault and a survivor's discovery of it
(Section~\ref{subsec:mas-resilience}) --- needs a joint fault$\times$comm
sweep with per-step trajectory logging; the same logging would deliver the two
emergence signatures (inter-drone distance over time, post-fault re-covering
latency) that Section~\ref{subsec:exp-qualitative} left qualitative. Two
further ablations --- removing the believed-alive count from the context
vector, and switching the shared terminal reward for an individual one ---
require retraining a policy each. None of these changes the architecture; they
are instrumentation and compute, and they would sharpen the claims already
made.

\paragraph{Richer coordination mechanisms.}
The finding that communication contributes little on this task
(Section~\ref{subsec:exp-ablations}) is partly a property of the task and
partly of the design. Replacing fixed map fusion with \emph{learned} messages
--- a differentiable communication channel the policy optimizes end-to-end ---
would let the system decide \emph{what} to share, and a task that genuinely
rewarded information sharing (a target visible only from afar, heterogeneous
sensors, or partial views that must be combined) would exercise that channel
far harder than target-in-a-maze search does. On the credit-assignment side,
value-decomposition methods (VDN/QMIX-style) would replace the shared-terminal
heuristic of Section~\ref{subsec:train-correctness} with a learned
factorization of team value into per-agent contributions.

\paragraph{Beyond the accumulated-map belief state.}
The hand-crafted knowledge maps that let a memoryless policy act in a
partially observable world (Section~\ref{subsec:prob-obs}) could be replaced
by a \emph{learned} memory --- a recurrent or attention-based encoder over the
observation history --- which would remove the assumption that a spatial
summary is a sufficient statistic and might capture temporal structure the
maps discard.

\paragraph{A harder, more realistic world.}
Every limitation of Section~\ref{subsec:exp-discussion} is a research
direction: moving or evasive targets; correlated or passage-blocking faults;
and, most pointedly given the ethical analysis, an \emph{adversary} that
chooses when and whom to disable and how to jam communication --- turning the
benign i.i.d.\ fault process into a strategic one and the cooperative game
into a mixed one. Finally, the whole study is simulation-only: continuous
dynamics, sensor and localization noise, and communication latency and loss
all stand between this grid world and a physical swarm, and closing that
sim-to-real gap --- while preserving the fault tolerance that is the point of
the exercise --- is the direction in which the work would have to move to
matter outside the laboratory.
