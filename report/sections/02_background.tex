\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section reviews the concepts the rest of the report relies on: the
single-agent reinforcement-learning toolbox
(Sections~\ref{subsec:bg-mdp}--\ref{subsec:bg-dqn}), its extension to
multiple interacting learners (Section~\ref{subsec:bg-marl}), and the
technique --- domain randomization with context conditioning --- that lets a
single policy serve many task variants (Section~\ref{subsec:bg-dr}). Readers
familiar with deep value-based reinforcement learning may skim through and
return to individual definitions as they are referenced later; no project
detail appears here.

\subsection{Sequential decision making}
\label{subsec:bg-mdp}

The standard formal model of an agent acting in an environment is the
\emph{Markov decision process} (MDP)
\citep{sutton2018reinforcement}, a tuple
$\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma \rangle$ where $\mathcal{S}$
is a set of states, $\mathcal{A}$ a set of actions,
$P(s' \mid s, a)$ the transition kernel, $R(s,a)$ the expected immediate
reward, and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ a discount factor. At each step the agent
observes the state $s_t$, picks an action $a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_t)$
according to its \emph{policy} $\pi$, receives a reward $r_t$ and moves to
$s_{t+1}$. The quantity being optimized is the expected \emph{return}, the
discounted sum of future rewards
$G_t = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k r_{t+k}$. The Markov property --- the
distribution of $(s_{t+1}, r_t)$ depends on the history only through
$(s_t, a_t)$ --- is what makes the problem tractable: it licenses value
functions defined on states rather than on histories.

Two value functions summarize how good states and actions are under a
policy: the state value $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s]$ and
the action value
$Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a]$. An optimal policy
$\pi^*$ maximizes both simultaneously, and its action-value function $Q^*$
satisfies the Bellman optimality equation
\[
Q^*(s,a) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\, r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a')
\;\Bigm|\; s, a \Bigr].
\]
Once $Q^*$ is known, acting optimally is trivial:
$\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)$.

\emph{Q-learning} \citep{watkins1992qlearning} estimates $Q^*$ from
interaction alone, without knowledge of $P$ or $R$. After each transition
$(s, a, r, s')$ it nudges the current estimate toward a bootstrapped target:
\[
Q(s,a) \;\leftarrow\; Q(s,a) + \alpha \Bigl[\, r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')
- Q(s,a) \Bigr],
\]
with step size $\alpha$. Q-learning is \emph{off-policy}: the target uses
the greedy action $\max_{a'}$ regardless of how $a$ was chosen, so the
experience-generating (behaviour) policy may explore freely. The standard
behaviour policy is \emph{$\varepsilon$-greedy}: with probability
$\varepsilon$ act uniformly at random, otherwise act greedily with respect
to the current $Q$; annealing $\varepsilon$ over training shifts the agent
from exploration to exploitation. In finite MDPs, tabular Q-learning
converges to $Q^*$ under mild conditions on visitation and step sizes
\citep{watkins1992qlearning}.

\subsection{Deep value-based reinforcement learning}
\label{subsec:bg-dqn}

Tabular methods require storing one value per state--action pair, which is
hopeless when states are high-dimensional (in this project, stacks of
$32\times32$ spatial maps). \emph{Function approximation} replaces the table
with a parameterized estimator $Q_\theta(s,a)$ --- in deep reinforcement
learning, a neural network --- trained by regression on bootstrapped
targets. The naive combination of Q-learning with deep networks is unstable:
consecutive samples are strongly correlated, and the regression target moves
with every update of $\theta$, since the same network appears on both sides
of the Bellman equation.

The \emph{Deep Q-Network} (DQN) \citep{mnih2015human} stabilizes this
combination with two mechanisms. \emph{Experience replay}
\citep{lin1992self} stores transitions in a large circular buffer and trains
on mini-batches sampled uniformly at random, breaking temporal correlations
and reusing each experience many times. A separate \emph{target network}
with frozen parameters $\theta^-$ --- periodically synchronized with the
online parameters $\theta$ --- supplies the bootstrap value, keeping the
regression target fixed between synchronizations. The loss for a sampled
transition is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\;
\ell\bigl(\, y - Q_\theta(s,a) \,\bigr),
\qquad
y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s', a'),
\]
where $\ell$ is typically the Huber loss, which behaves quadratically near
zero and linearly in the tails, limiting the influence of outlier targets.

\paragraph{Double DQN.}
The $\max$ operator in the target both \emph{selects} and \emph{evaluates}
the next action with the same (noisy) estimator, which systematically
overestimates values \citep{vanhasselt2016double}. \emph{Double DQN}
decouples the two roles: the online network selects, the target network
evaluates,
\[
y \;=\; r + \gamma\, Q_{\theta^-}\bigl(s',\,
\arg\max_{a'} Q_{\theta}(s', a')\bigr).
\]
This one-line change measurably reduces overestimation bias and improves
final policies at negligible cost.

\paragraph{Dueling architecture.}
The \emph{dueling} network \citep{wang2016dueling} decomposes the action
value into a state value and action \emph{advantages}, computed by two heads
on a shared representation:
\[
Q(s,a) \;=\; V(s) + A(s,a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
\sum_{a'} A(s,a') .
\]
The mean-subtraction resolves the identifiability of the decomposition
(adding a constant to $V$ and subtracting it from all $A$ leaves $Q$
unchanged) and, practically, lets the network learn the value of a state
without having to learn the effect of every action in it --- useful when, in
many states, the choice of action barely matters (e.g.\ open space far from
anything of interest), while in a few it is critical.

\paragraph{$n$-step returns.}
One-step targets propagate reward information a single transition at a time.
An \emph{$n$-step return} accumulates real rewards over a window of $m$
steps before bootstrapping:
\[
R_t^{(m)} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k},
\qquad
y = R_t^{(m)} + \gamma^m\, Q_{\theta^-}\bigl(s_{t+m},\, \cdot\,\bigr),
\]
trading a little extra variance (and, off-policy, a formally uncorrected
sampling mismatch that works well in practice
\citep{hessel2018rainbow}) for much faster credit propagation --- a
sensible trade in tasks with sparse terminal rewards, where a success signal
must travel backwards across long trajectories.

\paragraph{Termination vs.\ truncation.}
A subtlety that becomes central in this project
(Section~\ref{subsec:train-correctness}): when a window ends in a
\emph{terminal} state, the bootstrap term is dropped
($\gamma^m \to 0$) --- there is no future to estimate. But when an episode
is merely \emph{truncated} (e.g.\ by an external time limit), the future
does exist, the agent simply stops observing it; the correct target still
bootstraps from the last state \citep{pardo2018time}. Conflating the two
teaches the network that time-outs are value-zero events, systematically
biasing value estimates in time-limited tasks.

\subsection{Multi-agent reinforcement learning}
\label{subsec:bg-marl}

When several agents learn and act in the same environment, the MDP
generalizes to the \emph{Markov game} (or stochastic game)
\citep{littman1994markov}: a tuple
$\langle \mathcal{I}, \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, P,
\{R_i\}, \gamma \rangle$ with agent set
$\mathcal{I} = \{1,\dots,N\}$, where the transition kernel
$P(s' \mid s, a_1, \dots, a_N)$ and each agent's reward
$R_i(s, a_1, \dots, a_N)$ depend on the \emph{joint} action. Settings are
classified by reward structure: \emph{cooperative} (identical or aligned
interests), \emph{competitive} (zero-sum), and \emph{mixed}
(general-sum). Cooperative teams with a common objective --- the setting of
this project --- can in principle be treated as a single ``puppeteer''
agent over the joint action space, but that space grows exponentially in
$N$, and decentralized execution constraints usually forbid it anyway.

If, in addition, each agent perceives only part of the state, the model
becomes a \emph{decentralized POMDP} (Dec-POMDP)
\citep{oliehoek2016concise}: agents receive private observations
$o_i$ correlated with, but not determining, the state. Solving Dec-POMDPs
optimally is NEXP-complete \citep{bernstein2002complexity} --- a complexity
result that motivates the approximate, learning-based approaches used in
practice.

\paragraph{Non-stationarity.}
The central pathology of multi-agent learning is that, from any single
agent's perspective, the environment contains the \emph{other learning
agents}: as they update their policies, the transition and reward statistics
that agent~$i$ experiences drift, violating the stationarity assumption
underlying single-agent convergence guarantees
\citep{hernandezleal2019survey}. The two classical extremes span a
trade-off. \emph{Independent learners} \citep{tan1993multi} run a
single-agent algorithm per agent, ignoring the others: simple and scalable,
but the drifting environment can destabilize learning and silently erase
coordination. \emph{Joint-action learners} model the joint action space
explicitly: coordination-aware but exponential in $N$ and dependent on
observing others' actions.

\paragraph{Centralized training, decentralized execution.}
Most modern cooperative MARL adopts a middle path, \emph{centralized
training with decentralized execution} (CTDE)
\citep{kraemer2016multi,lowe2017multi}: during training --- typically in
simulation --- global information may be pooled (shared replay data, joint
critics, others' observations), but the artifacts that are deployed are
per-agent policies conditioned only on locally available inputs.
CTDE exploits the asymmetry between what is available in the laboratory and
what is available in the field, buying training-time stability without
sacrificing runtime decentralization.

\paragraph{Parameter sharing.}
In teams of homogeneous agents, a particularly effective CTDE instance is
\emph{parameter sharing} \citep{gupta2017cooperative}: all agents are driven
by a single network, trained on the pooled experience of the whole team.
Sharing multiplies the effective sample throughput per parameter, and
partially neutralizes non-stationarity at its source --- teammates cannot
drift apart when they are, parametrically, the same policy. Its known
caveat is symmetry: identically parameterized agents receiving identical
observations produce identical behaviour, so useful role differentiation
requires an input that distinguishes them (typically an agent index or
identifier appended to the observation).

\paragraph{Credit assignment.}
With a shared team reward, each agent faces the question of which part of
the outcome its own actions caused; a lazy agent can free-ride on a
successful teammate, and naive independent updates propagate the team's
success into everyone's values indiscriminately. Practical remedies range
from individually shaped reward components, which keep a per-agent learning
signal, to explicit value-decomposition schemes; the design tension is that
individual shaping can misalign incentives with the team objective if the
shaped terms dominate.

\paragraph{Partial observability and memory.}
Under partial observability the optimal policy generally depends on the
observation \emph{history}, not the last observation alone; the principled
object to condition on is a belief state
\citep{kaelbling1998planning}. Deep approaches either learn a recurrent
summary of the history \citep{hausknecht2015deep} or hand the agent an
explicitly engineered memory whose content is maintained outside the
network --- e.g.\ spatial maps accumulating everything observed so far ---
turning the observation itself into an approximate sufficient statistic of
the past. The latter choice trades representational generality for
trainability and transparency, and is the one taken in this project
(Section~\ref{subsec:prob-obs}).

\subsection{Generalization and domain randomization}
\label{subsec:bg-dr}

A policy trained in one fixed configuration tends to overfit it. When the
deployment conditions are uncertain --- or when one wants a \emph{single}
policy to cover a family of related tasks --- a standard remedy is
\emph{domain randomization} (DR): sampling the parameters that define the
task (in robotics, typically visual appearance or physical dynamics) anew
at every training episode, so that the learner experiences a distribution
of environments rather than a point
\citep{tobin2017domain,peng2018sim}. A policy that performs well across the
sampled distribution has, by construction, learned strategies robust to the
randomized factors; with wide enough ranges, the deployment condition
becomes ``just another sample''.

Randomization can be applied blindly --- the policy never knows which
variant it is in, and must behave robustly on average --- or with
\emph{context conditioning}: the sampled task parameters (or the subset of
them that a deployed agent could realistically know) are appended to the
policy input, turning the problem into a \emph{contextual} MDP
\citep{hallak2015contextual} and the network into a task-conditioned family
of policies with shared weights. Conditioning lets the same parameters
produce different behaviour in different regimes --- e.g.\ searching more
cautiously with a short sensing range than with a long one --- rather than a
single compromise behaviour. Which parameters to expose in the context and
which to leave latent is a design decision: observable, actionable
quantities help; quantities the agent could never know in reality would
train a dependence that breaks at deployment. This report returns to that
decision concretely in Section~\ref{subsec:arch-context}.
