\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the single shared policy trained in
Section~\ref{sec:training}. The questions are deliberately those the multi-agent
reading of Section~\ref{sec:mas} raised and left open: does one policy really
generalize across the randomization axes it was trained over; does it degrade
gracefully as random faults remove members of the team, the project's defining
scenario; and are the coordination regularities claimed as emergent visible in
aggregate behaviour? We first fix the protocol and the comparison points, then
take the axes one at a time, and close with the limitations that bound every
claim made here.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{subsec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Policy under test.}
Unless stated otherwise, all results use the final checkpoint of the
$50{,}000$-episode run, evaluated \emph{greedily}
($\varepsilon = 0$), so faults, when present, are the only source of
stochasticity in an episode. The learning curve below additionally revisits the
periodic checkpoints of the same run.

\paragraph{One-factor-at-a-time protocol.}
The policy was randomized over five environment factors simultaneously, so the
natural evaluation is a controlled \emph{one-factor-at-a-time} (OFAT) sweep: we
vary one factor at a time and
hold the others at a fixed \emph{reference operating point}, namely team size
$\nagents = 3$, vision $\rvis = 3$, communication $\rcomm = 5$, obstacle density
$\rho = 0.2$, step budget $T = 200$ and no faults, chosen at the centre of the
training ranges of Table~\ref{tab:notation}. This maps each axis without the
exponential cost of a full grid; the two interactions we do examine jointly are
reported as heatmaps in Section~\ref{subsec:exp-qualitative}. Each configuration
point is evaluated on the \emph{same} bank of $500$ seeds (learning-curve and
heatmap points use $300$); a seed fixes the obstacle layout, target, spawn corner
and, under faults, the entire fault sequence, so points differ only in the factor
being swept. In every plot the range actually seen in training is drawn on a
white background and the extrapolation beyond it (\emph{out-of-distribution},
OOD) on a shaded one.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report the task-level metrics defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:prob-objective}, plus a standard path-quality measure.
\emph{Success rate} is the fraction of episodes reaching the target within
$T\cdot\nagents$ agent-turns, with a Wilson $95\%$ confidence interval for the
binomial proportion. \emph{Time-to-find} is the number of \emph{rounds} elapsed
at success, averaged over successful episodes only. The simulator advances in
agent-turns and every drone consumes one turn per round, wrecks included since a
broken drone's no-op still costs its turn, so the raw count is divided by
$\nagents$. This reports elapsed mission time rather than accumulated drone
effort, and puts the metric in the same unit as the step budget $T$, against
which it would otherwise be incomparable. \emph{Coverage} is the fraction of free
cells the team has discovered at episode end; \emph{overlap} is the fraction of
team-visited cells visited by more than one drone, and \emph{revisits} the mean
per-drone re-entries, the two redundancy signatures promised in
Section~\ref{subsec:mas-emergence}. \emph{SPL} (Success weighted by Path Length)
\citep{anderson2018evaluation} weights each success by the ratio of the
optimal spawn-to-target distance to the finder's actual path length, rewarding
\emph{short} solutions and scoring failures zero; it is our single scalar for
path quality.

\paragraph{A note on episode return.}
The shaped return of Table~\ref{tab:reward} is reported alongside these metrics
but is \emph{not} used to rank configurations, and under faults it is actively
misleading: a drone that breaks stops paying the per-turn step penalty, so
removing team members can \emph{raise} the mean return even as fewer targets are
found. This is precisely the shaping-versus-task discrepancy that motivated the
success-dominant checkpoint rule of Section~\ref{subsec:train-correctness}; we
therefore read success rate, not return, as the primary metric throughout.

At the reference operating point the policy reaches the target in $87.6\%$ of
episodes (Wilson CI $[84.4, 90.2]$), discovering $53.7\%$ of the free space with
an SPL of $0.541$: the anchor every one-factor sweep below passes through. One
plotting convention holds throughout: bounded panels are drawn on axes fitted to
the data plus the confidence band, so that trends of a few points remain legible;
map coverage is the exception and keeps the full $0\text{--}100\%$ range, its
variation along the flatter axes being comparable to the sampling noise at $500$
seeds.

\subsection{Baselines and reference points}
\label{subsec:exp-baselines}

Two comparison points frame the results, neither of which is an upper bound.

\paragraph{The single drone (value of cooperation).}
The $\nagents = 1$ end of the team-size sweep is the natural ``no cooperation''
baseline: the same policy, deprived of any teammate to coordinate with. It
isolates what the team buys over a lone searcher and is discussed with the sweep
below.

\paragraph{BFS auto-navigation (a hybrid skyline).}
The evaluation-only override of Section~\ref{subsec:arch-tooling} replaces the
learned action by the first step of a breadth-first shortest path, on the drone's
\emph{own known map}, whenever that drone already knows where the target is. It
is a hybrid, since the learned policy still does all the \emph{searching} and the
planner only optimizes the final \emph{approach} once the target has been found,
and it is not an oracle: it plans on partial knowledge, and it cannot help an
episode in which no drone ever sees the target. We use it to separate two
competences the success rate conflates: finding the target, and travelling to it
efficiently. A gap in favour of auto-nav localizes a weakness in the policy's
homing, not in its search.

A uniform-random controller was not run as a separate condition: on a
$\gridsize\times\gridsize$ map with a single target and a step budget its success
is a floor far below every operating point reported here, and the single-drone
and auto-nav references are the informative comparisons.

Table~\ref{tab:generalization} summarizes all five OFAT axes; we read them in
three groups.

\paragraph{Team size: cooperation helps, and extrapolates.}
Adding drones raises success monotonically, from $71.0\%$ for a lone searcher to
$89.2\%$ at $\nagents = 4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep-nagents}), and, the result
worth stressing, the curve does not break when the team grows \emph{past} any
size seen in training: $\nagents = 5$ and $6$ reach $89.6\%$ and $90.4\%$. A
single shared network, conditioned only on the scalar team size and a per-drone
identity, thus drives teams larger than it ever trained on without retraining or
architectural change, which is the concrete pay-off of parameter sharing plus
context conditioning argued in Section~\ref{subsec:mas-learning}. SPL climbs
alongside success ($0.33 \to 0.58$ over $1\!\to\!4$, continuing to $0.64$ at
$\nagents = 6$): more searchers means the target is typically found by whichever
drone is already nearest, shortening the winning path.

Larger teams are also genuinely \emph{faster}, and not merely more likely to
succeed. Mission time falls from $84.9$ rounds for a lone searcher to $55.5$ at
$\nagents = 3$ and $50.4$ at $\nagents = 4$, continuing to $42.7$ at
$\nagents = 6$. The speed-up is real but markedly sublinear: $1.53\times$ at
$\nagents = 3$ and $1.99\times$ at $\nagents = 6$, a parallel efficiency falling
from $51\%$ to $33\%$. Doubling the team from three drones to six therefore cuts
mission time by a further $23\%$ rather than halving it again, which is the
quantitative form of the coordination overhead the next paragraph makes
concrete.

Two costs temper the picture, both visible in Figure~\ref{fig:sweep-nagents}.
Mean return \emph{falls} as the team grows, since more drones each pay the step
penalty, which is why return is not our ranking metric. And same-cell collisions
rise steeply with $\nagents$, an unavoidable consequence of a shared spawn corner
and a bounded map, where more bodies contend for the same early cells. Congestion
is precisely the mechanism behind the diminishing returns reported above: an
added searcher spends an increasing share of the episode contending for, and
re-sweeping, ground the team already holds. The two redundancy statistics
separate the individual from the collective view of this: per-drone revisits fall
and then flatten, each drone individually more efficient, while team overlap
rises ($18.5\% \to 37.0\%$ from $\nagents = 2$ to $6$), the team collectively
more wasteful. Coverage confirms the ceiling: it plateaus at
$53.7\text{--}55.2\%$ from $\nagents = 3$ on, so beyond that point extra drones
mostly duplicate each other's ground rather than extend the swept region.

\paragraph{Vision and communication: robust, with a telling asymmetry.}
Neither sensing axis has a cliff, in or out of distribution
(Table~\ref{tab:generalization}), but the two are robust in quite different ways.
Vision genuinely helps, and on every quality metric at once
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweep-vision}): from $\rvis = 1$ to $\rvis = 6$ coverage rises
from $46.5\%$ to $62.9\%$ (reaching $69.5\%$ at the OOD radius $\rvis = 8$), SPL
nearly doubles ($0.33 \to 0.63$), and mission time falls from $81.5$ rounds to
$43.0$, close to a halving. What saturates is the \emph{find rate}: success
climbs steeply out of myopia ($70.6\%$ at $\rvis = 1$ to $87.6\%$ at
$\rvis = 3$), peaks at $88.2\%$ around $\rvis = 4$, then drifts gently down in
and out of distribution alike, because past that point the bottleneck is deciding
\emph{where} to go rather than seeing farther. A wider window therefore keeps
buying speed and path quality long after it has stopped buying missions.

Communication is the more interesting axis, because there the success curve
really is flat: $87\text{--}90\%$ across the entire range, and severing fusion
outright ($\rcomm = 0$, an OOD probe) still yields $87.6\%$. What communication
buys is efficiency, and it buys a lot of it: between $\rcomm = 0$ and
$\rcomm = 10$, SPL rises from $0.492$ to $0.578$ and mission time falls from
$61.7$ rounds to $49.6$, each close to a fifth, while redundancy drops as shared
maps let drones avoid re-covering each other's ground. The asymmetry, then, is
that vision governs both \emph{whether} and \emph{how well} the team searches,
while communication governs only \emph{how well}. We take the latter up directly
as an ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:exp-ablations}, as it bears on the
coordination analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:mas-coordination}.

\paragraph{Density and budget: the difficulty axes.}
Obstacle density is the one factor that dominates the task
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweep-density}). Success falls smoothly from near-perfect in
open fields ($99.0\%$ at $\rho = 0$, $96.2\%$ at the trained floor
$\rho = 0.10$) to $53.4\%$ at the trained ceiling $\rho = 0.30$, and then
collapses out-of-distribution to $34.6\%$ and $24.2\%$ at $\rho = 0.35, 0.40$,
the only axis whose extrapolation fails outright. The mechanism is legible in the
companion panels: as clutter rises, corridors narrow, drones are funnelled
together (collisions and per-drone revisits climb sharply), coverage drops, and
SPL falls as detours lengthen the winning path. Time-to-find is the one panel
there not to read as a difficulty signal: averaged over successful episodes only,
its \emph{drop} at the OOD densities ($66.4$ rounds at $\rho = 0.30$ against
$21.1$ at $\rho = 0.40$) is survivorship, since at $24.2\%$ success the few
episodes that still finish are those whose target happened to lie near the spawn
corner. The step budget behaves as
expected of a pure time constraint: success rises with $T$ ($49.0\%$ at $T=50$ to
$89.8\%$ at $T=300$) with diminishing returns, since a generous budget only helps
episodes that were close to succeeding anyway.

\paragraph{Learning curve.}
Re-evaluating the periodic checkpoints at the reference operating point
(Figure~\ref{fig:epoch}) shows success rising to a
plateau around $80\text{--}85\%$ by roughly episode $25{,}000$ and holding, with
SPL continuing to inch up afterward: the policy keeps refining \emph{how} it
reaches targets after the \emph{whether} has largely converged. The intermediate
dip around episodes $17{,}500\text{--}22{,}500$ (success $68\text{--}70\%$) falls
in the stretch where exploration has long since floored, $\varepsilon$ hitting
$0.05$ at episode $5{,}990$, while the learning rate is still
$67\text{--}82\%$ of its peak on the cosine schedule: large updates against a
nearly greedy, self-generated state distribution. It recovers without
intervention as the annealing proceeds, and the greedy evaluation logged online
during the run itself, which uses a different instance bank and was recorded
independently of these re-evaluations, places its own local minima in the same
stretch, at episodes $16{,}000$ and $21{,}000$. This curve is the greedy,
$300$-seed complement to the online training scalars of
Figure~\ref{fig:training-curves}.

\subsection{Fault robustness}
\label{subsec:exp-faults}

This is the project's headline experiment: how does a policy trained over
$\pfault \in [0, 0.003]$ behave as drones are actually lost mid-mission?
Figure~\ref{fig:fault} and Table~\ref{tab:fault} sweep the per-turn fault
probability from the fault-free reference out to $\pfault = 0.01$, more than
triple the trained maximum: a regime in which, over $200$ rounds, a drone's
survival probability is only $0.99^{200} \approx 0.13$ and, at the reference team
size $\nagents = 3$, roughly $1.5$ drones are lost per episode on average.

\paragraph{Graceful degradation.}
The central result is that degradation is gentle and monotone. Across the
\emph{entire} trained range the success rate falls only from $87.6\%$ to
$82.0\%$, under six points, even though by $\pfault = 0.003$ the team is losing,
on average, $0.58$ of its three drones per episode. Pushed to the $3.3\times$ OOD
point $\pfault = 0.01$, success degrades to $66.2\%$ with $1.51$ drones lost: the
policy sheds, on average, \emph{half} the team and still completes two missions
in three. There is no cliff. This is the redundancy argued in
Section~\ref{subsec:mas-resilience} made quantitative, since any surviving drone
can complete the search, so the loss of some drones removes capacity rather than
the mission; and it holds under a fault rate the policy was never trained on,
evidence that the decentralized fault handling generalizes rather than memorizing
the training range.

\paragraph{Return rises while success falls.}
Table~\ref{tab:fault} and the training scalars make the shaping artefact
concrete: mean return \emph{rises} across the whole trained fault range
($71.2 \to 86.4$, peaking at $89.8$ before ever-shorter episodes pull it back
down) because wrecked drones stop incurring step penalties. Read naively, the
reward says faults \emph{help}; read correctly, success says they hurt, slowly.
The experiment is thus also a validation of the success-dominant
model-selection rule of Section~\ref{subsec:train-correctness}: had we selected
checkpoints on return, we would have preferred policies that let drones die
quietly.

\paragraph{What the aggregate hides: how many drones actually die.}
The mean loss counts of Table~\ref{tab:fault} average over very different
episodes, so Figure~\ref{fig:nbroken} opens them up. Panel~(a) shows the full
composition: even at the trained maximum $\pfault = 0.003$, a majority of
episodes ($59\%$) still lose \emph{no} drone, and total wipe-outs are rare
($3\%$); at the $3.3\times$ OOD point the picture inverts, with only $24\%$ of
episodes fault-free and $27\%$ losing the entire team. Panel~(b) conditions
success on how many drones survived, and this is where the graceful-degradation
claim becomes mechanical rather than rhetorical. A team that finishes intact
succeeds $93.9\%$ of the time; losing one drone costs $14$ points ($79.8\%$), and
even a lone survivor still finds the target in $52.6\%$ of episodes, roughly the
single-drone baseline reached from a worse starting position. The $0\%$ bar at
zero survivors is definitional, not a policy failure: an all-dead team truncates
the episode, so those $200$ episodes are lost to the environment rather than to
the policy.

\paragraph{Where the small gap lives.}
The auto-nav hybrid tracks the policy closely and a constant $2\text{--}3$ points
above it, at \emph{every} fault level (Figure~\ref{fig:fault}). Because auto-nav
changes only the homing phase, this near-constant offset says the policy's
residual weakness is a slightly sub-optimal final approach, not a failure to
handle faults, which would have widened the gap as $\pfault$ grew. Fault
tolerance is a property the learned policy already has; efficient homing is the
part a planner can still improve.

\paragraph{The belief-lag question.}
Section~\ref{subsec:mas-resilience} identified the interval between a fault and a
survivor's discovery of it, governed by $\rcomm$, as the system's ``window of
miscoordination''. Measuring it directly needs a joint $\pfault \times \rcomm$
sweep with per-step trajectory logs, which the present episode-summary harness
does not record; an indirect bound is available meanwhile. Since success is
nearly insensitive to $\rcomm$ even without faults, what fusion spreads being on
this task largely redundant with what a drone sees for itself, the additional
value of faster fault-news propagation is likely second-order for the find rate,
though it may matter more for path quality.

\subsection{Ablation: the role of communication}
\label{subsec:exp-ablations}

The communication axis doubles as a clean ablation, because $\rcomm = 0$ removes
map fusion entirely without retraining or changing anything else.
Figure~\ref{fig:sweep-comm} isolates it, and the result splits cleanly in two.
Fusion is not what lets the team \emph{find} the target: success is $87.6\%$ with
communication severed against $87\text{--}90\%$ over the whole range, a spread
well inside the confidence intervals. It is what lets the team find it
\emph{well}. Switching fusion on is worth close to a fifth of the team's
efficiency on every measure the harness records. Between $\rcomm = 0$ and the
trained maximum $\rcomm = 12$, SPL rises from $0.492$ to $0.587$ ($+19\%$),
per-drone revisits fall from $9.9$ to $8.6$ ($-13\%$) and inter-drone overlap
from $26.6\%$ to $21.0\%$ ($-21\%$), while mission time drops from $61.7$ rounds
to $49.6$ at its best ($-20\%$). For a mechanism that costs nothing
beyond being within range of a teammate, and that no reward term ever instructed
the policy to exploit, a uniform fifth off the cost of a mission is a substantial
return.

The reading matters for the multi-agent analysis. Communication is not what
\emph{creates} coordination here: with the channel cut the team still disperses
and still succeeds, which says that the dispersion itself is carried by the
\emph{environment-mediated, stigmergic} channel of
Section~\ref{subsec:mas-coordination}, drones spreading out because regions they
or their teammates have swept are made unrewarding. What fusion adds is
efficiency, and it adds it through the same artefact, the accumulated map, rather
than through any protocol: two coordination channels sharing one representation,
complementary rather than redundant, which is as concrete an instance of the
coordination-without-protocols theme of Section~\ref{sec:mas} as the system
offers. The honest caveat runs the other way from the usual one: because the find
rate is insensitive here, this sweep \emph{understates} what fusion would be
worth on a task that genuinely required information sharing (a target visible
only from afar, say, or heterogeneous sensing), where it would have to carry
success and not merely efficiency.

Two further ablations named in the analysis, removing $\hat n_i$ from the context
vector and switching the shared terminal reward for an individual one, change the
\emph{training} objective and so require retraining a policy each; they are
beyond the single-checkpoint scope of this evaluation and are noted as future
work in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

\subsection{Emergent coordination signatures}
\label{subsec:exp-qualitative}

Section~\ref{subsec:mas-emergence} claimed three measurable signatures of
emergent division of labour and committed to testing them here: complementary
coverage (low inter-drone overlap), inter-drone distance distributions over time,
and post-fault re-covering latency. We report the first, which the
episode-summary metrics capture directly, and are explicit about the two that
require trajectory-level logging.

\paragraph{Complementary coverage is real and quantified.}
Across the team-size sweep, the overlap between drones' swept regions stays low:
at $\nagents = 4$ only $29.6\%$ of team-visited cells are entered by more than
one drone, so roughly seven in ten cells are each the responsibility of a single
searcher. More tellingly, \emph{per-drone} revisits fall as the team grows, from
$13.9$ at $\nagents = 1$ to about $8.8$ from $\nagents = 4$ on, where they
flatten, even though total collisions rise: each drone individually wastes less
effort re-treading its own ground as the map is partitioned among more searchers.
This is exactly the complementarity predicted from local rules alone, no region
ever being assigned, and it is the aggregate footprint of the
identity-conditioned sector partition the shared policy learned.

\paragraph{Two interactions, read as heatmaps.}
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} confirms on $2$-D grids what the OFAT sweeps imply
marginally. Team size $\times$ communication (left) shows success governed almost
entirely by $\nagents$, with the $\rcomm$ direction nearly flat at every team
size: the communication finding above, now visibly independent of team size.
Vision $\times$ density (right) shows the reverse: density sets the achievable
success band and vision helps appreciably only in open maps, where seeing farther
translates into faster coverage, while in dense clutter the extra sight lines are
wasted on walls. The two dominant factors of the whole study, how \emph{many}
drones and how \emph{cluttered} the world, are exactly the two that these
interaction grids single out.

\paragraph{What we do not yet measure.}
The other two signatures, how pairwise inter-drone distances evolve within an
episode (the radial-dispersion claim) and the latency with which survivors
re-cover a wrecked drone's sector, are properties of the \emph{joint trajectory
over time}, and the current harness records only end-of-episode aggregates;
measuring them needs per-step position logging and a re-run of the fault sweep
with trajectories retained. The emergence claims therefore rest, at present, on the
coverage-complementarity signature above and on qualitative checkpoint
inspection, and no further.

\subsection{Discussion and limitations}
\label{subsec:exp-discussion}

The evaluation supports a bounded but clear claim: a single shared policy,
trained with domain randomization and context conditioning, generalizes across
team size, sensing and communication (including beyond its trained ranges),
degrades gracefully under a fault process it only partially saw in training, and
exhibits at least one measurable signature of emergent division of labour. The
limits of that claim should be stated as plainly as the claim itself, since
Section~\ref{sec:ethics} leans on them.

\emph{Simulation and abstraction.} Everything here is a $\gridsize\times
\gridsize$ occupancy grid with four-connected moves, noiseless local sensing and
instantaneous range-limited fusion. Continuous dynamics, sensor noise,
localization error, communication latency and packet loss, each of which would
stress the very mechanisms (map fusion, beacon detection) the policy relies on,
are absent, and no sim-to-real transfer is claimed or tested.

\emph{The fault model is benign.} Faults are i.i.d.\ across drones and turns,
independent of state and action, and produce inert, non-blocking wrecks.
Correlated failures, faults that block passage, and an \emph{adversary} choosing
when and whom to disable are all outside the model, and an adversary is exactly
the case the security discussion of the next section must contend with.

\emph{Statistical, not formal, evidence.} Success rates on $500$ seeds with
Wilson intervals bound sampling error, not worst-case behaviour: nothing here
certifies how the opaque learned policy acts on a specific unseen instance, a gap
Section~\ref{subsec:eth-responsibility} returns to. The OOD probes show graceful
extrapolation on the axes tested but do not guarantee it in general, density
already showing where extrapolation fails.

\emph{Instrumentation gaps.} The belief-lag / fault$\times$comm interaction and
two of the three emergence signatures were defined but not measured, for want of
per-step trajectory logging, and the two training-objective ablations were scoped
out for want of additional training runs. None of this undermines the results
reported; they mark where the next iteration of the evaluation would sharpen
them.
